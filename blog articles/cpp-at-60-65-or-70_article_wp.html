Retirement Transition Series · Part 1

When to start the Canada Pension Plan (CPP) is the question we hear most from people nearing retirement. The choice is permanent, and it shapes your guaranteed income for life.

Don't decide based on your neighbour

Most people hear about CPP timing from a friend or colleague, and the advice is usually to take it as soon as you can.

"A lot of people say, oh, my neighbour said, start CPP at 60. And that's not always the best option."

Heidi Blondin, CFP®, EPC

Starting CPP early can be the right call, but it really depends on your personal situation. There are a number of factors to consider when determining the right timing. There isn’t a one size fits all solution.

What you get at each start age

You can start CPP any time between age 60 and 70. Each month before 65 cuts your pension benefit by 0.6%; each month deferred after 65 adds 0.7%. Both adjustments will affect your income for the rest of your life. Here is what that means for someone entitled to $1,000 a month at 65:

"Starting CPP at 60, you take a 36% reduction for the rest of your life. That's a big hit, especially when you don't have any other guaranteed retirement income."

Heidi Blondin, CFP®, EPC

Why waiting matters most if you don't have a pension

Our view is that CPP timing matters most for people without a workplace pension. For them, CPP may be the only income they will have for life that is guaranteed, predictable and indexed to inflation.

"In a lot of cases, it's the only guaranteed, predictable income they'll have for the rest of their life. It's indexed, which means it grows a little bit each year depending on the cost of living."

Heidi Blondin, CFP®, EPC

Savings can deplete quickly; a larger CPP cheque does not. Drawing on savings for a few years while you delay CPP can buy a bigger guaranteed base income for the rest of your life.

Hypothetical illustration

Which pays more in total? It depends on how long you live. Using the example above, if you start at 60, you collect $640 a month for five years before someone who waits until 65 gets their first cheque. That is a head start of about $38,000.

From 65 on, the person who waited gets $360 more every month. By about age 74, they have caught up, and every year after that they pull further ahead. Waiting from 65 to 70 works the same way, with a catch-up age of about 82.

So starting early pays more if your retirement is shorter, and waiting pays more if it is longer.

(Simplified: these figures leave out tax and inflation.)

When starting earlier can make sense

Starting before 65 can be reasonable when:

  • You have stopped working and would otherwise draw heavily on savings or carry debt.
  • You have health concerns that make a shorter retirement more likely.
  • You have a solid defined benefit pension, so CPP is a smaller share of your guaranteed income.
  • You are already collecting a CPP spousal death benefit.

If you are still working, CPP is taxable at your marginal tax rate which may be higher while you still have employment income. Waiting until age 65 or beyond usually makes more sense if you are still working, have little or no pension, or expect a long retirement.

Look at the whole picture first

Pensions, OAS, RRSPs, LIRAs, TFSAs, Non-Registered savings, part-time work and a spouse's income sources all affect when CPP should start.

"The answer's not consistent for everybody, but it does matter. We have to look at the whole picture to see what makes most sense for each individual."

Heidi Blondin, CFP®, EPC
