The Launch Series · Part 2

Your account is open. Now comes the question that stalls most new investors: what actually goes inside it?

There is a whole range to choose from. At one end sit high-interest savings accounts and GICs, where the money does not move. At the other end sits full exposure to the stock market. Everything else is some mix of the two.

Choosing where you land on that range is not a test of how brave you are. It comes down to one question: when do you need the money?

"It's important to align the goal and the time frame with the investment option chosen."

Heidi Blondin, CFP

Time frames are general guidance, not a rule. Your own mix depends on the goal, your income, and how much fluctuation you can genuinely live with. Investment values are not guaranteed and will change over time.

Under three years? Stay low.

If the money has a job coming up soon, a car, a move, first and last month's rent, the only thing that matters is that it's there when you reach for it. That means a high-interest savings account or a GIC.

You will not get rich this way. You also will not go to withdraw it two weeks before you need it and find a chunk of it missing.

Longer than that? Time changes the math.

If you will not touch the money for ten years or more, the calculation flips. A bad year becomes an inconvenience instead of a disaster, because you have the runway to recover from it. That is the case for more equity exposure, and it's why your twenties are the best stretch of your life to hold it.

"New investors sometimes take too little risk, because it's all new. But the benefit of time is really on their side."

Heidi Blondin, CFP

Playing it too safe feels responsible. Over thirty years, it quietly costs you: money parked in a savings account for decades can lose ground to inflation even while the balance goes up.

The part nobody should skip

There is no guarantee in market-based investing. Some years your account will be worth less than what you put in. That is not a malfunction; it's the normal texture of it.

Growth is real. It just doesn't arrive in a straight line.

Be honest with yourself here

Both lines end in the same place. The only difference is that one of them is survivable and the other one is a fantasy. You have to be comfortable with some degree of fluctuation, because staying invested through the dips is what earns the growth. If a 20% drop would make you sell, that belongs in the plan from day one, and there is nothing wrong with saying so.
